Sheep's wool as a bedding fill: measured thermal conductivity, moisture transport and unpublished comparisons

Circadian · 2026-10-12

This entry gives the measured heat figures for sheep's wool used as a bedding fill. It states the method behind each one and what each leaves out. It keeps peer-reviewed results apart from maker figures. It also lists the comparisons nobody has published.

Measured and stated figures

Property Value Source type Condition or limit
Thermal conductivity 0.0333 to 0.0438 W/(m·K) Peer-reviewed testing on sheep's wool Varies with packing density; lower density traps more still air
Comparison with hemp fiber Wool insulated better at equivalent low densities Same study Only hemp was compared; no other fibers at this rigor
Moisture vapor uptake Up to 30 percent of its own weight Manufacturer product pages Not an independent test; describes uptake before the surface reads wet
Sleep onset versus cotton, cool rooms Shorter with wool bedding Systematic review, Journal of Sleep Research Attributed to higher water-vapor permeability and moisture buffering
Sleep onset, warm rooms Improved with wool bedding Same review Reported for older adults specifically

Still air in the crimp

Wool fiber grows with a natural wave, called crimp, that keeps fibers from packing flat. The gaps hold still air, a poor conductor of heat, which is what keeps a sleeper warm in a cold room. The peer-reviewed measurement above is a range. Conductivity rises as the fibers pack tighter and the air between them shrinks. This effect does not depend on humidity. It works all year.

Moisture vapor transport

Wool is hygroscopic: it draws water vapor out of the surrounding air and off skin. When humidity under the covers rises, from sweat or breath, the fiber takes the vapor in and releases it outward. The heat leaves with the moisture, so the layer keeps shedding warmth and the sleeper stays cool. In a cold, dry room there is little vapor to move, and the still air does most of the work. In a humid room or on a night with more sweating, vapor transport takes a larger share.

How this differs from cotton and polyester

Cotton and polyester fills insulate at roughly the same rate whether the air is dry or humid. Wool's behavior changes with the conditions around it. That difference is the property the Journal of Sleep Research review links to its sleep onset findings.

Variables that move the figures

Variable Effect Direction
Packing density Changes still-air volume per unit of fill Denser packing raises conductivity, so the fill insulates less per unit
Scouring, bleaching, dyeing Affect how much crimp and lanolin (the sheep's own oils) survive into the finished fill Heavier processing can reduce both insulation structure and moisture uptake
Blending with synthetic fiber Dilutes both mechanisms Reduces moisture uptake and crimp structure
Fill weight Weakly linked to warmth on its own A dense, heavily processed fill can insulate less than a lighter, minimally processed one
Machine washing or dry cleaning the fill Flattens the crimp Permanently reduces insulation; wash the cover only, spot clean the fill, air it in shade

Comparisons that have not been published

No peer-reviewed source found for this entry ranks wool against cashmere, alpaca or down with the method used for the wool and hemp figures. So this literature does not support calling wool the warmest natural fiber. What the evidence supports is narrower: wool pairs measured insulation with moisture vapor transport, and that pairing is what the sleep studies examined.

What these figures do not tell you

  • Conductivity is a property of the fill material, not of a finished pillow or duvet with a cover, stitching and a particular fill weight.
  • The 30 percent uptake figure is a manufacturer statement and should be read as one.
  • The warm-room sleep onset result applies to older adults and should not be generalized to all sleepers.
  • Perceived temperature at first contact is not measured by any figure here. Wool does not feel cold to the touch the way a phase-change gel does; its moisture response builds over the night.
