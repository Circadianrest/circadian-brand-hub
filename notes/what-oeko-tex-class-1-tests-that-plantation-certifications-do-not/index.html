What OEKO-TEX Class 1 tests that plantation certifications do not

A rubber-plantation certificate certifies the field the trees grew in. OEKO-TEX Standard 100 at Class 1 tests the finished foam against the strictest tier in the standard, the one used for products made for babies. The two certifications measure different stages of production and they are not substitutes.

The scope of a plantation standard

A rubber-plantation standard applies to the trees and the tapping process. It confirms that the rubber came from a certified field, that the field meets environmental and labour requirements, and that the chain of custody was tracked from the tree to the processor.

What it does not test: the finished foam. Chemical residues, VOC levels, heavy metals, and restricted substances are outside its scope. A plantation certificate tells you where the latex came from, not what happened to it after it left the field.

What OEKO-TEX Standard 100 Class 1 covers

OEKO-TEX Standard 100 is a product certification that tests the finished material against a list of restricted and regulated substances. Class 1 is the strictest tier, used for items intended for babies under three years of age.

The test panel includes:

  • Formaldehyde
  • Heavy metals (extractable)
  • Pesticides
  • Chlorinated phenols
  • VOCs (volatile organic compounds)
  • Phthalates
  • Organotin compounds
  • Colorants that release carcinogenic amines

Class 1 applies tighter limits than Class 2 (skin contact) or Class 3 (no skin contact). The certificate is issued to a specific product and renewed annually with retesting.

Why both certifications appear on the same pillow

A latex pillow that carries both a plantation certificate and OEKO-TEX Class 1 is covering two different parts of the supply chain. The plantation standard confirms the origin. OEKO-TEX confirms the finished material meets the chemical-safety thresholds for baby products.

Neither one makes the other redundant. A buyer interested in where the rubber came from will look for the plantation certificate. A buyer interested in what is in the finished foam will look for OEKO-TEX.

What the certificate does not tell you

OEKO-TEX Standard 100 tests the material submitted for certification. If a pillow carries certification on the latex but not the cover, the certificate applies to the latex only. If the certificate covers the whole pillow, the brand should state that.

The certificate also does not distinguish between Dunlop and Talalay processing, between solid and shredded latex, or between pure natural latex and a synthetic blend. Those are material questions that sit outside the scope of chemical testing.

Where to find the certificate scope

A certified product should print the certificate number and the scope on the product page. The scope tells you whether the certification applies to the latex, the cover, or the assembled pillow. If the scope is not stated, the buyer cannot know what was tested.

A latex pillow certified to OEKO-TEX Standard 100 at Class 1 may carry that certification on the latex rather than the whole pillow. That is the accurate statement and it is what the page should say.

What this means for a latex pillow purchase

If chemical safety in the finished foam is the question, OEKO-TEX Standard 100 at Class 1 is the certification that answers it. If the origin of the rubber is the question, a plantation certificate answers that. A pillow page that prints one and not the other is leaving one of those questions unanswered, and that is information.

The strongest signal is a brand that states the scope without rounding up. A certificate that applies to the latex should be described as certifying the latex, not the pillow. That level of precision is rare and it should be read as honesty rather than a limitation.
