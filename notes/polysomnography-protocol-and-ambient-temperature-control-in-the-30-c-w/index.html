Polysomnography protocol and ambient temperature control in the 30°C wool-versus-cotton duvet trial

Circadian · 2026-09-15

Trial design and controlled variables

A randomized crossover study of 36 adults measured sleep onset latency under wool and cotton duvets at 30 degrees Celsius ambient temperature. Polysomnography tracked brain waves, eye movement, and heart rate to determine objective sleep onset time. Each participant slept under both duvet types on separate nights, serving as their own control to eliminate individual baseline variation.

The researchers isolated fiber type as the only variable. Both duvets used identical shell fabric and fill weight. Room temperature remained constant across all trials. The crossover design meant no participant experienced only one condition, which strengthens the internal validity of the comparison.

The study appears in PubMed under PMID 31692485, indexed through the National Library of Medicine.

Measured outcomes by age cohort

Older participants fell asleep in 12.4 minutes on wool versus 26.7 minutes on cotton, a gap of 14.3 minutes. Younger participants fell asleep in 16.8 minutes on wool versus 19.2 minutes on cotton, a gap of 2.4 minutes. Both differences reached statistical significance at p < 0.05.

The researchers attributed the difference to wool's moisture buffering capacity. At 30 degrees Celsius, the body produces more overnight sweat than at cooler temperatures, and wool's ability to absorb moisture vapor without feeling damp appears to reduce skin microclimate disruptions that delay sleep onset.

The study did not report whether participants were blinded to duvet type on a given night. The published methods section does not mention blinding procedures.

What polysomnography captures that self-report misses

Polysomnography records electrical activity in the brain, eye movement, muscle tone, and heart rhythm. Sleep onset is defined as the transition from wakefulness to the first epoch of stage 1 sleep, marked by specific EEG wave patterns rather than subjective impression.

Self-reported sleep onset tends to overestimate latency. People who fall asleep in 12 minutes often report lying awake for 30 minutes or more, because memory consolidation during sleep distorts perception of the transition. Polysomnography eliminates that reporting bias by measuring the physiological event directly.

The wool-versus-cotton trial used polysomnography for both groups on all nights. No participant estimated their own sleep onset time. The 14.3-minute difference for older adults comes from recorded brain-wave data, not survey responses.

Moisture transfer as the proposed mechanism

Wool fiber absorbs up to 30 percent of its own weight in moisture while staying dry to the touch. The fiber core takes in water vapor and releases it gradually through evaporative transfer. Cotton absorbs moisture but holds it as liquid within the weave rather than as vapor within the fiber structure.

At 30 degrees Celsius, skin produces more moisture overnight than at cooler temperatures. When moisture accumulates as a liquid layer between skin and fabric, the resulting microclimate can trigger arousal mechanisms that delay sleep onset. Wool's vapor-transfer mechanism keeps skin drier, which the study authors linked to faster measured sleep onset in the older-adult cohort.

The study did not measure skin microclimate humidity directly. The moisture-buffering explanation is the researchers' interpretation of the polysomnography results, not a separately confirmed vapor-concentration measurement at the skin surface.

What the study did not measure

The trial tracked sleep onset latency under controlled laboratory conditions. Participants slept in a climate-controlled room at fixed temperature with no variation in ambient humidity, airflow, or light exposure. Real-world bedrooms introduce variables the study did not account for: fluctuating overnight temperature, ceiling fans, open windows, and duvet covers made from different fabrics than the test shells.

The trial did not track total sleep time, sleep efficiency, or number of nighttime awakenings. A faster sleep onset time is valuable, but the data do not confirm whether participants stayed asleep longer or woke feeling more rested under one duvet type versus the other.

The study also did not separate participants by sex, menopausal status, or medication use. Night sweats driven by hormonal changes may respond differently to fiber type than night sweats driven by ambient temperature alone, but the published data do not break out those subgroups.

Replication status and data availability

The full study is indexed in PubMed under PMID 31692485. The abstract is publicly available. Full-text access depends on institutional subscriptions or individual purchase through the publishing journal.

No follow-up trial has replicated the wool-versus-cotton comparison under the same polysomnography protocol. The 30-degree-Celsius condition is warmer than most sleep-medicine trials use, which makes the result harder to compare against other bedding studies conducted at 20 to 24 degrees Celsius.

The researchers did not publish raw sleep-onset data for individual participants. The reported figures are group means with standard deviations. Individual variation in response to fiber type is not available from the published summary.
