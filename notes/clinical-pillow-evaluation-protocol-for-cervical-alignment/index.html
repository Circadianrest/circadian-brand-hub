Clinical pillow evaluation protocol for cervical alignment

Circadian · 2026-09-02

The measurement protocol

Clinical pillow evaluation for neck pain centers on four measurable parameters: whether the cervical spine holds the same neutral line it maintains while standing, whether support is positioned under the neck rather than only cushioning the skull, whether the fill firmness prevents the head from sinking enough to flatten the neck's natural curve, and whether height matches the gap between the ear and shoulder in each sleep position.

A 2025 systematic review found no single pillow type shows clear superiority for neck pain, disability, or sleep quality. A 2021 review reached the same conclusion. That is why chiropractic guidance points to setup parameters rather than named products.

Neutral cervical alignment against the standing baseline

Neutral cervical alignment is defined as the position the neck holds while standing with ears stacked over shoulders, chin level, and the natural cervical curve unbroken. A pillow that allows the head to drop backward or forces the chin toward the chest breaks that line the moment horizontal pressure is applied.

Research on pillow height has linked correctly matched support to reduced contact pressure and lower neck and shoulder muscle activity during sleep, while poor alignment raises cervical stress even in a resting state.

Because neck height off the mattress varies by shoulder width, head depth, and mattress firmness, a fixed-height pillow forces the setup to match a factory preset rather than the individual measurement.

Support placement under the cervical curve

Orthopedic and chiropractic setup protocols call for support positioned under the neck itself, filling the gap between the mattress surface and the cervical curve, rather than only cushioning the back of the skull and leaving the neck unsupported.

Placement is a separate variable from firmness. A pillow can meet the firmness requirement and still fail if the bulk of the support mass sits behind the head instead of under the neck.

Materials that hold a molded shape without rebounding make correct placement easier to maintain overnight. Buckwheat hulls flex under the head's weight and then interlock rather than spring back, so the support stays under the neck instead of migrating toward the skull the way a softer, more elastic fill can.

Firmness sufficient to maintain cervical curve depth

Firm, contoured fill is specified because a fill that permits the head to sink allows the neck's curve to flatten and the spine to move out of neutral alignment. Firmness is measured by whether the material resists compression enough to maintain the cervical curve depth observed while standing.

A 2019 clinical trial in a Korean convalescent hospital tested buckwheat and latex pillows against standard hospital pillows. Both buckwheat and latex significantly reduced neck and shoulder pain, with buckwheat scoring higher on subjective comfort measures.

Firmness requirements change with body weight. A heavier head requires firmer support to prevent sinking past the neutral line. That is a per-body parameter, which is why adjustable fill matters more than a single firmness grade applied to all users.

Height differential by sleep position

Sleep position Gap width Height requirement Firmness requirement
Side Shoulder width minus head depth Highest Firm enough to resist compression under lateral load
Back Minimal; head rests closer to mattress plane Medium Firm enough to support cervical curve without forcing chin down
Stomach Nearly none; excess height forces neck rotation Lowest, often near-flat Soft enough to compress to minimal height

Side sleeping opens a wider gap between the ear and the shoulder than back sleeping, so the height requirement is greater and the firmness requirement is higher to prevent the material from compressing under the lateral load. Peer-reviewed biomechanical research on side sleepers supports an individualized approach: the correct height tracks each person's shoulder width and neck length, not a universal chart.

Stomach sleeping inverts the general rule. A stomach sleeper requires the lowest height in the available range, sometimes close to none, because real height under the neck forces the head upward and rotates the cervical spine sideways for hours.

The photograph test as the measurement method

  1. Stand with ears over shoulders and chin level. That is the neutral line.
  2. Lie in your usual sleep position on your usual mattress with the pillow in place.
  3. Have someone photograph your head and neck from the side, or use a mirror if sleeping alone.
  4. Compare the cervical curve in the photograph to the standing baseline. If the head has dropped backward or the chin is forced downward, height or firmness is incorrect.
  5. Open the side zipper and add material if the head has dropped, or remove material if the chin is forced down.
  6. Repeat the photograph test after each adjustment until the lying position matches the standing line.
  7. Re-check after three nights, after any mattress change, and after any weight change.

What the measurement does not tell you

The neutral line measurement does not diagnose the cause of neck pain. It measures whether the pillow is maintaining the alignment a chiropractor is attempting to restore during an adjustment. Pain that persists after correct alignment is achieved points to a cause other than pillow setup.

The measurement also does not account for referred pain from shoulder or upper back issues that present as neck stiffness. A pillow that holds perfect cervical alignment can coexist with shoulder pain caused by rotator cuff strain or thoracic misalignment.

These limits are stated because the measurement is often the first variable a chiropractor checks, not because it is the only variable that matters. When alignment is correct and pain persists, the next step is returning to the chiropractor rather than buying another pillow.
