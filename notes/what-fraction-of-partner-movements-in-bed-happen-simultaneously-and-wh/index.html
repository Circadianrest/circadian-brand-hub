What fraction of partner movements in bed happen simultaneously, and why it matters for shared bedding

Circadian · 2026-10-21

The Loughborough movement study

Researchers at Loughborough University fitted 46 couples with movement monitors and tracked them for 15 nights at home. The study, published in Sleep in 1994, recorded every movement each partner made during the night.

About a third of the movements one partner made happened at the same moment as a movement from the other partner. The coupling was higher on nights the couples shared a bed compared to nights sleeping alone. Women in the study were also more likely to name their partner as the thing that woke them during the night.

The study used actigraphy, which records motion through a wrist-worn device. It does not distinguish between the cause of the movement and the response to it, so the one-third figure includes both simultaneous independent movements and movements triggered by the partner's motion.

How a shared comforter couples the movements

A single comforter stretched across two bodies passes motion from one side to the other. When one sleeper rolls over, the textile layer on top of the other person shifts with them. A mattress absorbs some of that motion. A shared comforter carries the rest straight through.

Two separate comforters break the coupling. A turn on one side stays on that side. The person next to you keeps the coverage they set, and the layer does not pull away when you move.

The metabolic rate gap

A 2015 study published in Nature Climate Change measured the metabolic rate of young women doing light desk work. The figure came in 20 to 32 percent below the value the standard thermal comfort model assumes.

Metabolic rate determines how much heat the body generates at rest. A lower rate means less heat output, which is why many women feel cold in a room set to a temperature their male partner finds comfortable. The gap has a physiological base.

One comforter has one weight and one warmth. Two people with different metabolic rates need different layers.

Twin comforters on a queen or king bed

Two Twin comforters cover the width of a queen or king mattress. A Twin sewn to 72 by 90 inches covers half of a 60-inch queen with room to tuck under each side and overlap in the middle. On a king, two Twins meet across the full width.

Each comforter goes in its own Twin duvet cover. Corner loops on the comforter tie to the cover so neither shifts to the foot overnight. The cover takes the washing. The comforter inside does not go in the machine.

The REM benefit of a shared bed

A 2020 study brought 12 couples into a sleep lab in Kiel, Germany for monitored nights. On nights the couples shared a bed, researchers recorded about 10 percent more REM sleep compared to nights sleeping alone. REM sleep was also less fragmented, and the couples' sleep stages aligned with each other more often.

The same study found an increase in leg movements on shared nights. The REM and synchronization benefits came from proximity. The movement penalty came from the physical coupling of a shared layer.

Two comforters eliminate the coupling. The bed stays shared, so the REM benefit remains.

What the method does not address

Two comforters solve the tug, reduce motion transfer, and let each sleeper set their own warmth. They do not fix noise, light, or schedule mismatch. The 2023 American Academy of Sleep Medicine survey found that more than a third of US adults sleep apart from a partner at least some of the time, and the reasons include snoring, different work schedules, and other factors beyond movement and temperature.

The movement coupling measured at Loughborough is the problem two comforters address directly.

Measurement notes

The Loughborough study used actigraphy on 46 couples over 15 nights in their own homes. The Kiel study was lab-based polysomnography on 12 couples, with four shared nights and four separate nights per couple. The metabolic rate study measured 16 young women at controlled ambient temperature during sedentary desk work. The one-third movement overlap figure does not isolate cause from response, so it includes both simultaneous independent movements and motion triggered by the partner.
